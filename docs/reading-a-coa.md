# Reading a certificate of analysis

## What is a certificate of analysis?

A certificate of analysis is a laboratory report covering one production lot. It records
what a laboratory measured in a sample of that lot, which methods it used, and how the
results compared to the acceptance criteria it was judged against.

A certificate covers exactly one lot. A document describing a product in general is a
specification sheet, not a certificate.

## Which four fields matter most?

**The lot identifier.** The batch code printed on the physical product. Without it, a
certificate cannot be matched to anything in hand. This is the single most common defect
in published certificates, and it makes the document decorative regardless of what the
results say.

**The testing laboratory.** A named laboratory can be looked up, and its accreditation
checked. An unnamed one makes every result on the page unverifiable.

**The analytes tested.** What was actually measured. A certificate showing three heavy
metals has not told you about the fourth.

**The date.** When the sample was tested, not only when the document was issued. Those are
different dates and the gap between them is informative.

## What does an accreditation mean?

ISO/IEC 17025 is the international standard for the competence of testing laboratories. A
laboratory accredited to it has been assessed by an accrediting body against defined
requirements for methods, equipment, personnel and quality systems.

Accreditation has an expiry date. A certificate whose testing occurred after the
laboratory's accreditation lapsed carries a weaker claim than its letterhead suggests.
The validator checks this ordering.

## How do audits and certificates differ?

They answer different questions and buyers routinely conflate them.

| | Manufacturing audit | Certificate of analysis |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Question answered | Was the process assessed against defined standards? | What did this specific lot contain when tested? |
| Scope | Facility, procedures, records | One lot, one sample |
| Frequency | Typically annual | Per lot |
| Tells you about a bag in your hand | No | Yes, if the lot number matches |

A manufacturer can hold one without the other. Neither is a government approval, an
endorsement, or a statement about what a product does.

## What do the units mean?

Mass fractions appear in several interchangeable forms, and confusing two of them is a
common and consequential error.

| Unit | Equal to |
| --- | --- |
| mg/kg | 1 ppm |
| µg/g | 1 ppm |
| µg/kg | 1 ppb |
| ppb | 0.001 ppm |
| mg/g | 1,000 ppm |
| 1 percent | 10,000 ppm |

A lead result of 900 ppb and a limit of 1.5 mg/kg look like a thousandfold violation and
are not one: 900 ppb is 0.9 mg/kg, comfortably within the limit. The validator converts
before comparing, and refuses to compare units that are genuinely incommensurable, such as
CFU/g against mg/kg.

## What does "below LOQ" mean?

The limit of quantitation is the lowest concentration a method can measure with acceptable
precision. The limit of detection is lower still: the concentration at which the method can
tell something is present without measuring how much.

A result below the limit of quantitation should be reported as *below LOQ*, not as a
number. Reporting a figure the method cannot reliably produce implies a precision that
does not exist. The limit of detection can never exceed the limit of quantitation; a
certificate stating otherwise has a transcription error.

## How should pathogens be reported?

Salmonella, *E. coli* and *Listeria* are presence/absence determinations, not counts. The
correct result is detected or not detected against an absence criterion.

A count for a pathogen indicates the wrong test was reported, or the wrong field was
filled in. An analyte detected against an absence criterion and marked as passing is a
direct contradiction.

## Which heavy metals are usually reported?

Lead, arsenic, cadmium and mercury. A heavy metals panel omitting one of the four is not
necessarily a problem — the omission may be deliberate and documented — but it is worth
noticing, because the four are conventionally reported together.

## What a certificate cannot tell you

It cannot tell you the sample was representative of the lot. It cannot tell you the
material in your hand came from the lot on the certificate, unless the lot codes match.
It cannot tell you anything about a different lot. And it says nothing whatsoever about
what a product does in a person.
